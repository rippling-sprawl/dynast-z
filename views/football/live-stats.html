Live Stats

Where the odds, props and box scores on this site come from, and how current they are.

The Short Version

Every NFL game on this site has its own page: the betting lines, the player props, the box score, and every play. Those pages are not live windows into somebody else's site — they are snapshots, taken on a schedule and stored here.

A robot checks each game and grabs a fresh snapshot. How often depends on how close the game is:

  • Weeks away — not at all. The sportsbooks haven't posted anything yet, so there is nothing to take a picture of.
  • The week of the game — every few hours.
  • The last few hours before kickoff — every ten minutes, because that is when the lines move most.
  • While the game is being played — every couple of minutes.
  • After the whistle — a few more times over the next six hours, in case the league corrects a stat. Then it stops forever, because a finished game never changes again.

Every game page tells you exactly where it stands in that cycle, at the top, next to the game's status: updated 4m ago · next in 6h.

So if a page looks stale, it probably isn't. It's just not due yet.

The Longer Version

Where the numbers come from

One source: balldontlie, a sports data company that sells access to the NFL feed. We pay for a plan, they give us a key, and that key lets a script ask questions like "what are the current lines on this game?" or "give me every play from Super Bowl LX."

It was picked over roughly seventeen alternatives for one boring reason that matters a lot: its terms of service explicitly allow storing the data and building something on top of it. Most sports feeds do not — they let you display their numbers and nothing else, which rules out keeping a history, and keeping a history is the entire point.

The schedule is the spine

The Games page lists all 272 regular-season games. That list also comes from balldontlie, and that is deliberate: because the schedule and the game data come from the same place, they use the same ID numbers. Clicking a row on the schedule and asking the API about that game are the same act. There is no translation step in the middle to get wrong.

Before this, the schedule came from ESPN, which meant two sets of ID numbers for the same 272 games and a permanent opportunity for a mismatch. Now there is one.

How a game page gets filled in

A snapshot is assembled from about a dozen separate questions to the API — current lines, opening lines, the player-prop board, injuries, game-day designations, team stats, individual stats, and every play. The answers are shaped into a single file and stored in a database.

When you open a game page, it loads that one file. That's why it's fast, and why it works the same whether the game is three weeks out or three years past. The page also quietly re-checks itself on the same schedule the robot runs, so a page left open through a kickoff keeps up on its own — no refreshing.

Snapshots live in a database rather than in the codebase because they're big: a single game is around 900 KB, almost all of it player props — about 34 players × 25 markets × both sides of every over/under. Keeping one per game in the repository would add roughly 15 MB a week during the season, permanently, for numbers that are stale within the hour.

Only two sportsbooks, on purpose

balldontlie quotes eight to ten books per game. The snapshots keep DraftKings and FanDuel. A line at a book you don't have an account with isn't a line you can act on, and dropping the rest cut each snapshot roughly in half. The others are one flag away if that changes.

Why some pages say "not captured yet"

Three different reasons, and the page will tell you which:

  • Too early. More than eight days out, nothing is captured because the books haven't posted. The page says roughly what date that changes.
  • Due, but not swept yet. It'll appear on the next pass, and the page will pick it up without a reload.
  • It finished before any of this existed. Games that are already over never change, so nothing re-checks them on a timer — the entire 2025 season is in that bucket. They get filled in deliberately, in batches, rather than by a robot discovering 272 of them at once.

Two honest limits

It depends on a subscription. The lines, props and play-by-play sit behind the top paid tier. If that lapses, the feed answers "no" to those questions and snapshots stop getting richer. There is a guard against the worse version of that: before storing a snapshot, the script compares it to the one already stored and refuses to replace a full one with an emptier one. A lapsed subscription makes the data stop updating; it can't make it disappear.

Live games need a machine that's awake. The scheduled robot runs on GitHub's free infrastructure, which is best-effort — it drifts five to fifteen minutes when they're busy. Fine for a Tuesday, useless for a two-minute live cadence. For a slate worth watching closely, the same sweep runs in a loop on a real computer and keeps up properly.

Everything is kept

Every single answer the API has ever given is saved to disk, exactly as it arrived. Two reasons. One: any snapshot can be rebuilt later without paying for the data twice. Two: closing lines are not for sale after the fact — balldontlie keeps opening lines but not closing ones, so a line nobody captured before kickoff is gone at any price. Capturing them as the game approaches is the only way to have them.
